## 1. Genesis of Periodic Classification & Modern Periodic Law


### 1.1 Historical Evolution & Pre-Modern Systems


1. **Dobereiner's Triads (1829):**
   Arranged elements in triads where the atomic weight of the central element was approximately the arithmetic mean of the weights of the other two:
   * $\text{Li} (7), \; \text{Na} (23), \; \text{K} (39) \implies \frac{7 + 39}{2} = 23$.
   * $\text{Ca} (40), \; \text{Sr} (88), \; \text{Ba} (137) \implies \frac{40 + 137}{2} = 88.5 \approx 88$.
   * $\text{Cl} (35.5), \; \text{Br} (80), \; \text{I} (127) \implies \frac{35.5 + 127}{2} = 81.25 \approx 80$.


2. **Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865):**
   Arranged elements by increasing atomic mass; every eighth element exhibited properties analogous to the first (similar to musical octaves).
   * *Limitation:* Valid only up to Calcium ($Z = 20$). Failed for heavier elements containing transition metals.


3. **Lothar Meyer's Atomic Volume Curves (1869):**
   Plotted atomic volume ($\text{Atomic Mass} / \text{Density}$) against atomic mass:
   * **Crests:** Alkali metals ($\text{Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs}$) possessing largest atomic volumes.
   * **Descending Slopes:** Alkaline earth metals ($\text{Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba}$).
   * **Ascending Slopes:** Halogens ($\text{F, Cl, Br, I}$).
   * **Troughs:** Transition elements (d-block metals) having highest densities and small volumes.


4. **Mendeleev's Periodic Table & Law (1869):**
   * *Law:* Physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their **atomic weights**.
   * *Merits:* Systematic group arrangement; left gaps predicting properties of undiscovered elements: **Eka-Aluminium** ($Ga$) and **Eka-Silicon** ($Ge$); corrected atomic weights (e.g., $\text{Be} = 9$, previously considered $13.5$).
   * *Demerits:* Position of Hydrogen; no place for isotopes; anomalous inverted atomic weight pairs: $\text{Ar} (39.94)$ before $\text{K} (39.10)$, $\text{Co} (58.93)$ before $\text{Ni} (58.71)$, $\text{Te} (127.6)$ before $\text{I} (126.9)$.


---


### 1.2 Moseley's Experiment & The Modern Periodic Law


In 1913, Henry Moseley bombarded various target metals with high-energy cathode rays and analyzed the frequencies ($\nu$) of the characteristic X-rays ($K_\alpha$ lines) emitted:


$$\mathbf{\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b)}$$


where $\nu$ is the frequency of characteristic X-rays, $Z$ is the atomic number (nuclear charge), and $a, b$ are screening constants.


* **Deduction:** A plot of $\sqrt{\nu}$ against $Z$ yielded a straight line, whereas $\sqrt{\nu}$ against atomic weight produced irregular curves.
* **Modern Periodic Law:**
  $$\mathbf{\text{The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their ATOMIC NUMBERS (Z).}}$$
* This resolution instantly justified the placement of Argon ($Z=18$) before Potassium ($Z=19$), Cobalt ($Z=27$) before Nickel ($Z=28$), and Tellurium ($Z=52$) before Iodine ($Z=53$).


---


### 1.3 Architecture of the Modern (Long Form) Periodic Table


1. **Periods (7 Horizontal Rows):**
   * Period 1: $n = 1 \implies 1s^2 \implies 2\text{ elements}$ (Very short).
   * Periods 2 & 3: $n = 2, 3 \implies s, p \implies 8\text{ elements each}$ (Short).
   * Periods 4 & 5: $n = 4, 5 \implies s, d, p \implies 18\text{ elements each}$ (Long).
   * Period 6: $n = 6 \implies s, f, d, p \implies 32\text{ elements}$ (Very long, includes 14 Lanthanides $4f^{1-14}$).
   * Period 7: $n = 7 \implies s, f, d, p \implies 32\text{ elements}$ (Includes 14 Actinides $5f^{1-14}$).


2. **Prediction of Period, Group, and Block from Configuration:**
   * **Period Number:** Maximum principal quantum number $n$ in the electronic configuration.
   * **Block:** Subshell receiving the differentiating (last) valence electron.
   * **Group Number:**
     * For **$s$-block:** Group $= \text{Number of valence } s\text{-electrons}$ ($1$ or $2$).
     * For **$p$-block:** Group $= 12 + \text{Number of valence } p\text{-electrons} = 10 + \text{valence electrons}$.
     * For **$d$-block:** Group $= (n - 1)d\text{-electrons} + ns\text{-electrons}$.
     * For **$f$-block:** **Always Group 3 (IIIB)** for all Lanthanides and Actinides!


3. **IUPAC Nomenclature for Super-Heavy Elements ($Z > 100$):**
   Numerical roots: $0 = \text{nil}, 1 = \text{un}, 2 = \text{bi}, 3 = \text{tri}, 4 = \text{quad}, 5 = \text{pent}, 6 = \text{hex}, 7 = \text{sept}, 8 = \text{oct}, 9 = \text{enn}$. Suffix: **-ium**.
   * $Z = 104$: Un + nil + quad + ium $\implies \text{Unnilquadium (Unq)}$.
   * $Z = 118$: Un + un + oct + ium $\implies \text{Ununoctium (Uuo)}$.

## 2. Shielding Effect & Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$)


### 2.1 The Concept of Screening ($\sigma$)


In a multi-electron atom, valence electrons experience two competing forces:
1. Electrostatic attraction toward the positively charged nucleus ($+Ze$).
2. Electrostatic repulsion from inner core electrons pushing the valence electron outward.


The net positive charge experienced by the valence electron is the **Effective Nuclear Charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$)**:


$$\mathbf{Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - \sigma}$$


where $\sigma$ is the **Screening / Shielding Constant**.


* **Screening Power Hierarchy:**
  $$\mathbf{s > p > d > f}$$
  Because $s$-orbitals are spherically symmetric and penetrate deeply toward the nucleus, they shield outer electrons most effectively. Conversely, $d$ and $f$ orbitals have diffused, multi-lobed spatial shapes, resulting in **very poor shielding**.


---


### 2.2 Slater's Rules for Calculating $\sigma$


To calculate $\sigma$ for an electron in an atom:
Group orbitals in order: $(1s) \, (2s, 2p) \, (3s, 3p) \, (3d) \, (4s, 4p) \, (4d) \, (4f) \dots$


1. **For an electron in an $ns$ or $np$ orbital:**
   * Other electrons in the same $(ns, np)$ group shield by $\mathbf{0.35}$ each (for $1s$, shield by $\mathbf{0.30}$).
   * Electrons in the penultimate shell $(n - 1)$ shield by $\mathbf{0.85}$ each.
   * Electrons in all deeper inner shells $(n - 2, n - 3, \dots)$ shield by $\mathbf{1.00}$ each.


2. **For an electron in an $nd$ or $nf$ orbital:**
   * Other electrons in the same $(nd)$ or $(nf)$ group shield by $\mathbf{0.35}$ each.
   * All electrons in all groups to the left (all inner shells) shield by $\mathbf{1.00}$ each.

## 3. Atomic & Ionic Radii: Periodic Gradients & Anomalies


![Periodic Trends Atomic Radius and Lanthanide Contraction](/media/periodic_trends_atomic_radius_and_lanthanide_contraction.webp)
*Description: Two-panel periodic architecture reference: (Panel A) Periodic Trends Master Compass detailing horizontal and vertical gradients of atomic radius, ionization enthalpy, electron affinity, and electronegativity; (Panel B) Lanthanide Contraction bar chart comparing $4d$ and $5d$ metallic radii showing near-exact matching ($        ext{Zr}  pprox         ext{Hf},         ext{Nb}  pprox         ext{Ta},         ext{Mo}  pprox         ext{W}$) and the $d$-block contraction in Group 13 ($        ext{Ga} <         ext{Al}$).*


### 3.1 Types of Radii & Structural Metric Hierarchy


1. **Covalent Radius ($r_{\text{cov}}$):** Half the internuclear distance between two covalently bonded identical atoms in a single homonuclear bond:
   $$r_{\text{cov}} = \frac{d_{A-A}}{2}$$
   * **Schomaker-Stevenson Equation for Heteronuclear Bonds:**
     $$\mathbf{d_{A-B} = r_A + r_B - 0.09 |\chi_A - \chi_B| \quad [\text{in \AA}]}$$
     where $\chi_A, \chi_B$ are Pauling electronegativities.


2. **Metallic Radius ($r_{\text{met}}$):** Half the internuclear distance between two adjacent metal ions in a metallic crystal lattice.


3. **Van der Waals Radius ($r_{\text{vdw}}$):** Half the internuclear distance between two non-bonded adjacent atoms belonging to neighboring molecules in the solid state.


$$\mathbf{r_{\text{Van der Waals}} > r_{\text{Metallic}} > r_{\text{Covalent}}}$$


* *Noble Gas Radius:* Noble gases do not form covalent molecules under normal conditions; their radii are recorded as Van der Waals radii, making them appear artificially larger than preceding halogens!


---


### 3.2 Lanthanide Contraction & Group 13 Contraction


1. **The Lanthanide Contraction:**
   Across the 14 Lanthanide elements ($_{58}\text{Ce}$ to $_{71}\text{Lu}$), electrons are added successively to the inner $4f$ subshell.
   * **Cause:** The shielding of $4f$ electrons is extremely poor ($\sigma_{4f} \ll \sigma_d \ll \sigma_s$). Consequently, the nuclear charge increases by $+14$ while the shielding increases very weakly.
   * **Result:** A steady, pronounced contraction of the electron cloud occurs.
   * **Major Chemical Consequences:**
     * The atomic and metallic radii of $4d$ and $5d$ transition elements in the same group are **almost identical**:
       $$\mathbf{\text{Zr} (1.60\text{ \AA}) \approx \text{Hf} (1.59\text{ \AA}), \quad \text{Nb} (1.46\text{ \AA}) \approx \text{Ta} (1.46\text{ \AA}), \quad \text{Mo} (1.39\text{ \AA}) \approx \text{W} (1.39\text{ \AA})}$$
     * The chemical properties of $\text{Zr}/\text{Hf}$ and $\text{Nb}/\text{Ta}$ are virtually identical, making chemical separation exceptionally difficult.
     * Basic strength of Lanthanide hydroxides decreases steadily: $\mathbf{\text{La(OH)}_3 > \text{Ce(OH)}_3 > \dots > \text{Lu(OH)}_3}$ as ionic size shrinks and covalent character rises.


2. **Transition Metal / $d$-Block Contraction in Group 13:**
   * Down Group 13, atomic radius is expected to increase: $\text{B} < \text{Al} < \text{Ga} < \text{In} < \text{Tl}$.
   * **Experimental Anomaly:**
     $$\mathbf{r_{\text{Ga}} (135\text{ pm}) < r_{\text{Al}} (143\text{ pm})}$$
   * **Cause:** Gallium ($_{31}\text{Ga}: [\text{Ar}]\, 3d^{10} 4s^2 4p^1$) is preceded by 10 transition elements filling the $3d$ subshell. The poor shielding of the 10 $3d$ electrons allows the $+31$ nuclear charge to pull the outer $4s, 4p$ electrons inward, causing Gallium's radius to contract below Aluminium.


---


### 3.3 Ionic Radii & Isoelectronic Sequences


1. **Cation vs. Parent Atom:**
   $$\mathbf{r_{\text{cation}} < r_{\text{atom}}}$$
   Loss of electrons increases the $Z/e$ ratio, increasing $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and drawing remaining electrons closer.
   * Higher oxidation state $\implies$ smaller radius: $\mathbf{\text{Fe}^{3+} (0.64\text{ \AA}) < \text{Fe}^{2+} (0.78\text{ \AA}) < \text{Fe} (1.26\text{ \AA})}$.


2. **Anion vs. Parent Atom:**
   $$\mathbf{r_{\text{anion}} > r_{\text{atom}}}$$
   Addition of electrons decreases the $Z/e$ ratio, increasing inter-electronic repulsions and expanding the electron cloud.
   * $\mathbf{\text{I}^- (2.16\text{ \AA}) > \text{I} (1.33\text{ \AA}) > \text{I}^+ (0.94\text{ \AA})}$.


3. **Isoelectronic Series (Same Number of Electrons):**
   $$\mathbf{\text{Ionic Radius} \propto \frac{1}{Z} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Nuclear Charge}}}$$
   For the 10-electron isoelectronic series:
   $$\mathbf{\text{N}^{3-} (1.71\text{ \AA}) > \text{O}^{2-} (1.40\text{ \AA}) > \text{F}^- (1.36\text{ \AA}) > \text{Na}^+ (0.95\text{ \AA}) > \text{Mg}^{2+} (0.65\text{ \AA}) > \text{Al}^{3+} (0.50\text{ \AA})}$$

## 4. Ionization Enthalpy ($\Delta_i H$ / IE)


![Ionization Energy and Electron Gain Enthalpy Anomalies](/media/ionization_energy_and_electron_gain_enthalpy_anomalies.webp)
*Description: Two-panel energetic property graphic: (Panel A) Period 2 first ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H_1$) zig-zag trajectory showing electronic configuration spikes at $        ext{Be} (2s^2) >         ext{B} (2p^1)$ and $        ext{N} (2p^3) >         ext{O} (2p^4)$; (Panel B) Bar chart of electron gain enthalpy magnitudes across halogens and chalcogens highlighting the $2p$ inter-electronic repulsion anomaly ($        ext{Cl} >         ext{F}$ and $        ext{S} >         ext{O}$).*


### 4.1 Definition & Fundamental Thermodynamics


Ionization enthalpy is the minimum energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated neutral gaseous atom in its ground state:


$$M(g) + \Delta_i H_1 \longrightarrow M^+(g) + e^-$$


* **Successive Ionization Enthalpies:**
  $$\mathbf{\Delta_i H_3 > \Delta_i H_2 > \Delta_i H_1}$$
  Removing an electron from a positive ion occurs against a significantly stronger effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$).
* **Predicting Valence Electrons from IE Jumps:**
  A dramatic leap in successive ionization energy indicates the transition from valence shell electrons to a stable noble gas core configuration!
  * If $\text{IE}_2 \gg \text{IE}_1 \implies$ Group 1 Alkali Metal (1 valence electron, e.g., Na).
  * If $\text{IE}_3 \gg \text{IE}_2 \implies$ Group 2 Alkaline Earth Metal (2 valence electrons, e.g., Mg).


---


### 4.2 Factors Influencing Ionization Enthalpy


1. **Nuclear Charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$):** $\text{IE} \propto Z_{\text{eff}}$.
2. **Atomic Radius ($r$):** $\text{IE} \propto \frac{1}{r}$.
3. **Penetration Effect of Orbitals:** For the same principal quantum number $n$:
   $$\mathbf{s > p > d > f}$$
4. **Electronic Configuration Stability:** Extraordinary stability of completely filled ($s^2, p^6, d^{10}$) and half-filled ($p^3, d^5$) subshells.


---


### 4.3 Anomalies Across Periods 2 & 3


1. **Period 2 Trend:**
   $$\mathbf{\text{Li} < \text{B} < \text{Be} < \text{C} < \text{O} < \text{N} < \text{F} < \text{Ne}}$$
   * **$\text{Be} > \text{B}$ Anomaly:**
     * $\text{Be}: [\text{He}]\, 2s^2$ (Completely filled, penetrative $2s$ subshell).
     * $\text{B}: [\text{He}]\, 2s^2 2p^1$ (Removing an electron from a higher-energy, less penetrative $2p$ subshell).
     * Hence, $\mathbf{\text{IE}_1(\text{Be}) = 899\text{ kJ/mol} > \text{IE}_1(\text{B}) = 801\text{ kJ/mol}}$.
   * **$\text{N} > \text{O}$ Anomaly:**
     * $\text{N}: [\text{He}]\, 2s^2 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$ (Symmetrical, half-filled stable configuration with high exchange energy).
     * $\text{O}: [\text{He}]\, 2s^2 2p_x^2 2p_y^1 2p_z^1$ (Presence of paired electrons in the $2p_x$ orbital causes inter-electronic repulsion; ionization relieves this pairing energy).
     * Hence, $\mathbf{\text{IE}_1(\text{N}) = 1402\text{ kJ/mol} > \text{IE}_1(\text{O}) = 1314\text{ kJ/mol}}$.


2. **Period 3 Analogous Trend:**
   $$\mathbf{\text{Na} < \text{Al} < \text{Mg} < \text{Si} < \text{S} < \text{P} < \text{Cl} < \text{Ar}}$$
   * $\text{Mg} (3s^2) > \text{Al} (3p^1)$ and $\text{P} (3p^3) > \text{S} (3p^4)$.


3. **Group 13 Ionization Enthalpy Irregularity:**
   Due to $d$-block and lanthanide contractions, the ionization enthalpy down Group 13 follows the unusual order:
   $$\mathbf{\text{B} > \text{Tl} > \text{Ga} > \text{Al} > \text{In}}$$

## 5. Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{        ext{eg}} H$ / EA)


### 5.1 Enthalpy Change & Thermodynamics


Electron gain enthalpy is the enthalpy change accompanying the addition of an electron to an isolated neutral gaseous atom:


$$X(g) + e^- \longrightarrow X^-(g), \quad \Delta_{\text{eg}} H$$


1. **First Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}} H_1$):**
   * Usually **negative (exothermic)** for non-metals because the incoming electron experiences attraction toward the positive nucleus.
   * **Positive (Endothermic) Exceptions:** Noble gases ($ns^2 np^6$, stable octet), $\text{Be}, \text{Mg}$ ($ns^2$, full subshell), and $\text{N}$ ($2p^3$, half-filled stability).


2. **Second Electron Gain Enthalpy ($\Delta_{\text{eg}} H_2$):**
   * **ALWAYS POSITIVE (ENDOTHERMIC) FOR ALL ELEMENTS!**
   $$O^-(g) + e^- \longrightarrow O^{2-}(g) \quad (\Delta_{\text{eg}} H_2 = +780\text{ kJ/mol})$$
   The incoming electron experiences powerful electrostatic repulsion from the already negative anion, requiring substantial energy input to force electron addition.


---


### 5.2 The Period 2 vs. Period 3 Anomaly (Halogens & Chalcogens)


Down a group, $\Delta_{\text{eg}} H$ is expected to become less negative (less exothermic) as atomic radius increases:


1. **Halogens Group 17:**
   $$\mathbf{\text{Cl} (-349) > \text{F} (-328) > \text{Br} (-325) > \text{I} (-295\text{ kJ/mol})}$$
   * *Mechanism:* Fluorine has an extraordinarily small, compact $2p$ subshell ($r_F \approx 72\text{ pm}$) with dense electron clouds. The incoming electron encounters intense **inter-electronic repulsion**, lowering the net exothermic energy released. In Chlorine, the incoming electron enters the larger, more diffused $3p$ orbital, where repulsions are minimal.
   * **Result:** **Chlorine possesses the highest electron gain enthalpy (most exothermic) in the entire periodic table!**


2. **Chalcogens Group 16:**
   $$\mathbf{\text{S} (-200) > \text{Se} (-195) > \text{Te} (-190) > \text{Po} > \text{O} (-141\text{ kJ/mol})}$$
   * Oxygen has the **least negative** electron gain enthalpy in the entire group due to compact $2p$ repulsions!

## 6. Electronegativity (EN) Scales & Bond Polarity


![Electronegativity Scales and Inert Pair Effect](/media/electronegativity_scales_and_inert_pair_effect.webp)
*Description: Two-panel chemical periodicity graphic: (Panel A) Formulation architectures of the Pauling, Mulliken, and Allred-Rochow electronegativity scales with hybridization and oxidation state influences; (Panel B) Inert Pair Effect stability hierarchy for Groups 13, 14, and 15 illustrating the thermodynamic dominance of lower oxidation states in heavier congeners.*


Electronegativity is the qualitative tendency of an atom in a chemical molecule to attract shared bonding electron pairs toward itself. Unlike IE or EA, it is not an energetic property of an isolated atom.


### 6.1 Quantitative Electronegativity Scales


1. **Pauling Scale (Bond Dissociation Energy Method):**
   Based on the excess bond energy $\Delta_{A-B}$ arising from ionic resonance stabilization:
   $$\Delta_{A-B} = E_{A-B} - \sqrt{E_{A-A} \cdot E_{B-B}}$$
   $$\mathbf{\chi_A - \chi_B = 0.208 \sqrt{\Delta_{A-B}} \quad (\text{if energies in kcal/mol})}$$
   $$\mathbf{\chi_A - \chi_B = 0.1017 \sqrt{\Delta_{A-B}} \quad (\text{if energies in kJ/mol})}$$
   * Pauling set **$\chi_{\text{F}} = 4.0$** as the arbitrary universal benchmark.
   * Key Pauling values: $\text{F}(4.0) > \text{O}(3.5) > \text{Cl}(3.0) \approx \text{N}(3.0) > \text{Br}(2.8) > \text{I}(2.5) \approx \text{C}(2.5) \approx \text{S}(2.5) > \text{H}(2.1)$.


2. **Mulliken-Jaffe Scale (Absolute Scale):**
   Electronegativity is the arithmetic average of first ionization energy and electron affinity:
   $$\mathbf{\chi_M = \frac{\text{IE} + \text{EA}}{2} \quad [\text{in eV}]}$$
   * **Conversion to Pauling Scale:**
     $$\mathbf{\chi_P = \frac{\chi_M}{2.8} = \frac{\text{IE} + \text{EA}}{5.6} \quad [\text{in eV}]}$$
     $$\mathbf{\chi_P = \frac{\text{IE} + \text{EA}}{540} \quad [\text{in kJ/mol}]}$$


3. **Allred-Rochow Scale (Electrostatic Force Scale):**
   $$\mathbf{\chi_{\text{AR}} = 0.359 \left( \frac{Z_{\text{eff}}}{r_{\text{cov}}^2} \right) + 0.744 \quad [r \text{ in \AA}]}$$


---


### 6.2 Factors Affecting Electronegativity


1. **Hybridization State:**
   Electronegativity increases directly with the **fraction of $s$-character** in the hybrid orbital ($s$-orbitals are closer to the nucleus):
   $$\mathbf{sp (50\% s) > sp^2 (33.3\% s) > sp^3 (25\% s)}$$
2. **Oxidation State of the Element:**
   Higher oxidation state increases the positive charge on the central atom, increasing $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and electronegativity:
   $$\mathbf{\text{Fe}^{3+} > \text{Fe}^{2+}, \quad \text{Mn}^{7+} > \text{Mn}^{4+} > \text{Mn}^{2+}, \quad \text{Tl}^{3+} > \text{Tl}^+}$$

## 7. The Inert Pair Effect & Oxidation State Stability


### 7.1 Relativistic & Shielding Mechanism


**Definition:** The reluctance of the outermost valence $ns^2$ electrons to participate in chemical bonding in heavier post-transition elements of Groups 13, 14, and 15 (Period 6 elements: $\text{Tl, Pb, Bi}$).


* **Underlying Cause:**
  1. The $6s^2$ electrons are preceded by 14 inner $4f$ and 10 inner $5d$ electrons, which shield the nuclear charge very poorly.
  2. The high unshielded nuclear charge pulls the $6s$ orbital strongly inward (contracting it).
  3. The energy required to unpair and promote the $6s$ electrons to empty $p$-orbitals is large and not compensated by the bond energies of the two additional bonds.
* **Consequence:** The lower oxidation state (Group state $-2$) becomes progressively **more stable** down the group than the group oxidation state.


---


### 7.2 Group-Wise Stability Hierarchies & Redox Behaviors


1. **Group 13 ($+3$ vs. $+1$):**
   * $+3$ Stability: $\mathbf{B^{3+} > Al^{3+} > Ga^{3+} > In^{3+} > Tl^{3+}}$
   * $+1$ Stability: $\mathbf{Tl^+ > In^+ > Ga^+ > Al^+ > B^+}$
   * **Redox Implication:** $\text{Tl}^{3+}$ is a **powerful oxidizing agent**, readily reducing to stable $\text{Tl}^+$:
     $$\text{Tl}^{3+} + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Tl}^+ \quad (E^\circ = +1.26\text{ V})$$
   * $\text{TlCl}$ is stable and ionic; $\text{TlCl}_3$ decomposes spontaneously to $\text{TlCl} + \text{Cl}_2$ on heating.


2. **Group 14 ($+4$ vs. $+2$):**
   * $+4$ Stability: $\mathbf{C^{4+} > Si^{4+} > Ge^{4+} > Sn^{4+} > Pb^{4+}}$
   * $+2$ Stability: $\mathbf{Pb^{2+} > Sn^{2+} > Ge^{2+}}$
   * **Redox Implication:**
     * $\mathbf{\text{Pb}^{4+}}$ compounds are **strong oxidizing agents** (e.g., $\text{PbO}_2 + 4\text{HCl} \to \text{PbCl}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
     * $\mathbf{\text{Sn}^{2+}}$ compounds are **reducing agents** because $\text{Sn}^{4+}$ is more stable than $\text{Sn}^{2+}$ (e.g., $\text{SnCl}_2 + 2\text{FeCl}_3 \to \text{SnCl}_4 + 2\text{FeCl}_2$).


3. **Group 15 ($+5$ vs. $+3$):**
   * $+5$ Stability: $\mathbf{P^{5+} > As^{5+} > Sb^{5+} > Bi^{5+}}$
   * $+3$ Stability: $\mathbf{Bi^{3+} > Sb^{3+} > As^{3+}}$
   * **Redox Implication:** $\text{BiF}_5$ is the only known stable pentahalide of Bismuth; $\text{Bi}_2\text{O}_5$ is an unstable, violent oxidizing agent.

## 8. Periodicity of Oxides & Diagonal Relationships


### 8.1 Acidic, Basic & Amphoteric Nature of Oxides


The acid-base character of oxides is governed by the electronegativity and polarizing power of the central element:


1. **Across a Period (Left to Right):**
   $$\mathbf{\text{Basic} \longrightarrow \text{Amphoteric} \longrightarrow \text{Acidic}}$$
   * Period 3 Oxides:
     * $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$: Strongly Basic (dissolves to give $\text{NaOH}$).
     * $\text{MgO}$: Mildly Basic.
     * $\mathbf{\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3}$: **Amphoteric** (reacts with both acids and bases).
     * $\text{SiO}_2$: Weakly Acidic.
     * $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$: Moderately Acidic.
     * $\text{SO}_3$: Strongly Acidic.
     * $\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$: Very Strongly Acidic (anhydride of $\text{HClO}_4$).


2. **Down a Group:**
   Metallic character increases $\implies$ **Basic nature increases** (e.g., $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ acidic $\to \text{Bi}_2\text{O}_3$ basic).


3. **The Amphoteric Repertoire:**
   Oxides and hydroxides that react with both strong acids and strong alkalis:
   $$\mathbf{\text{BeO}, \; \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \; \text{ZnO}, \; \text{SnO}, \; \text{SnO}_2, \; \text{PbO}, \; \text{PbO}_2, \; \text{Ga}_2\text{O}_3, \; \text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3}$$
   * *Reactions of $\text{ZnO}$:*
     $$\text{ZnO} + 2\text{HCl} \longrightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{Basic reaction})$$
     $$\text{ZnO} + 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2[\text{Zn(OH)}_4] \quad (\text{Sodium Zincate, Acidic reaction})$$


4. **Neutral Oxides:**
   Do not form salts with either acids or bases:
   $$\mathbf{\text{CO}, \quad \text{NO}, \quad \text{N}_2\text{O}, \quad \text{OF}_2}$$

### 8.2 Diagonal Relationships & Polarizing Power ($\phi$)


Elements of Period 2 diagonally adjacent to Period 3 elements exhibit striking chemical similarities:
$$\mathbf{\text{Li} \sim \text{Mg}, \quad \text{Be} \sim \text{Al}, \quad \text{B} \sim \text{Si}}$$


* **Physical Cause:** Moving right across a period increases polarizing power; moving down a group decreases it. These two opposite effects cancel along the diagonal!
  $$\mathbf{\phi = \frac{\text{Ionic Charge}}{\text{Ionic Radius}} \approx \text{Constant along diagonal}}$$
* **Salient Similarities ($        ext{Be} \sim \text{Al}$):**
  * Both $\text{BeO}$ and $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ are amphoteric.
  * Both $\text{Be}_2\text{C}$ and $\text{Al}_4\text{C}_3$ are methanides (yield $\text{CH}_4$ on hydrolysis).
  * Both $\text{BeCl}_2$ and $\text{AlCl}_3$ have polymeric/dimeric bridged structures in solid/vapor states and act as Lewis acids.

## 9. High-Yield JEE Traps & Problem-Solving Pitfalls


1. **The Halogen Electron Affinity Inversion Trap:**
   * In halogens, electronegativity is $\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$.
   * But electron gain enthalpy is **$\text{Cl} > \text{F} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$**! Never equate electronegativity with electron affinity.
2. **Successive Ionization Jump Identification:**
   * If $\text{IE}_1 = 500, \text{IE}_2 = 4500, \text{IE}_3 = 6900\text{ kJ/mol}$, the element has **1 valence electron** (alkali metal), not 2! The jump occurs *after* the valence electron is stripped.
3. **The Inert Pair Oxidation vs. Reduction Trap:**
   * $\text{Pb}^{4+}$ wants to gain electrons to become $\text{Pb}^{2+} \implies \mathbf{\text{Pb}^{4+} \text{ is an OXIDIZING agent}}$.
   * $\text{Sn}^{2+}$ wants to lose electrons to become $\text{Sn}^{4+} \implies \mathbf{\text{Sn}^{2+} \text{ is a REDUCING agent}}$.
4. **Second Electron Gain Enthalpy Sign Trap:**
   * $\text{O} + e^- \to \text{O}^-$ is exothermic (negative).
   * But $\text{O} + 2e^- \to \text{O}^{2-}$ is **overall ENDOTHERMIC (+)** because $\Delta_{\text{eg}} H_2 (+780\text{ kJ/mol}) > |\Delta_{\text{eg}} H_1 (-141\text{ kJ/mol})|$!
