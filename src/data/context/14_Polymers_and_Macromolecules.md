1. Classification of Polymers

1.1 Fundamental Definitions

- Polymer: High molecular mass macromolecule ($10^3\text{ to } 10^7\text{ u}$) composed of repeating structural units derived from simple reactive molecules called monomers.
- Degree of Polymerization ($n$): The total number of repeating monomeric units in a polymer chain.

1.2 Classification Based on Origin

2. Natural Polymers: Found in plants and animals (e.g., starch, cellulose, proteins, nucleic acids, natural rubber).
3. Semi-Synthetic Polymers: Chemically modified natural polymers (e.g., cellulose diacetate / rayon, cellulose nitrate / gun cotton, vulcanized rubber).
4. Synthetic Polymers: Man-made laboratory/industrial polymers synthesized from low molecular mass hydrocarbons (e.g., polythene, nylon, polystyrene, teflon, bakelite).

1.3 Classification Based on Intermolecular Forces

- The mechanical and thermal properties of polymers depend directly on intermolecular cohesion:
   1. Elastomers: Possess the weakest intermolecular forces (van der Waals). Chains are randomly coiled and hold together by light chemical cross-links, permitting high extensibility and reversible retraction (e.g., vulcanized rubber, Buna-S, Buna-N, neoprene).
   2. Thermoplastics: Possess intermediate intermolecular forces (stronger than elastomers, weaker than fibres). Linear or slightly branched chains without cross-links; soften reversibly on heating and harden on cooling (e.g., polythene, polystyrene, PVC, Teflon).
   3. Fibres: Possess strong intermolecular forces (primarily hydrogen bonding and strong dipole-dipole interactions). Long, thread-like linear chains with high tensile strength and high modulus (e.g., Nylon-6,6, Terylene/Dacron, Polyacrylonitrile/Orlon).
   4. Thermosetting Polymers: Possess extensive 3D covalent cross-links formed permanently on heating. Infusible, insoluble, and cannot be remolded (e.g., Bakelite, Melamine-formaldehyde, Urea-formaldehyde).

1.4 Visual Preservation: Intermolecular Forces Spectrum

 Description: Relative hierarchy of intermolecular cohesion across the four primary polymer classes, showing the progression from weakest van der Waals interactions in elastomers, through intermediate dipole attractions in thermoplastics, up to strong hydrogen-bonding in fibres and permanent 3D covalent cross-linking in thermosetting resins.

1.5 Classification Based on Synthesis Mechanism

5. Addition (Chain-Growth) Polymers: Formed by repeated addition of unsaturated monomer molecules without the elimination of any byproduct: $$n (\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2) \to -(\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_2)_n-$$
6. Condensation (Step-Growth) Polymers: Formed by repeated condensation reactions between bi-functional or poly-functional monomers with the elimination of small byproduct molecules like $\text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{HCl}, \text{CH}_3\text{OH}$: $$n \text{H}_2\text{N-(CH}_2)_6\text{-NH}_2 + n \text{HOOC-(CH}_2)_4\text{-COOH} \to -[\text{HN-(CH}_2)_6\text{-NH-CO-(CH}_2)_4\text{-CO}]_n- + (2n-1)\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

7. Addition Polymerization & Rubbers

2.1 Free-Radical Mechanism of Addition Polymerization

- Initiators: Peroxides (e.g., benzoyl peroxide, acetyl peroxide, tert-butyl peroxide).
   1. Chain Initiation: $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-CO-O-O-CO-C}_6\text{H}_5 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2 \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-CO-O}^\bullet \to 2 \text{C}_6\text{H}_5^\bullet + 2 \text{CO}_2$$ $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5^\bullet + \text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2^\bullet$$
   2. Chain Propagation: $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2^\bullet + n (\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2) \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_5-(\text{CH}_2\text{-CH}_2)_n\text{-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2^\bullet$$
   3. Chain Termination: Occurs via combination of two growing radicals or disproportionation.

2.2 Important Addition Homopolymers

- Low-Density Polythene (LDPE): Prepared under high pressure ($1000-2000\text{ atm}$) and $350-570\text{ K}$; highly branched structure, flexible, poor conductor (used in squeeze bottles, insulation).
- High-Density Polythene (HDPE): Prepared under low pressure ($6-7\text{ atm}$) and $333-343\text{ K}$ using Ziegler-Natta Catalyst ($\text{TiCl}_4 + \text{Al(C}_2\text{H}_5)_3$); linear structure, high density and tensile strength (used in buckets, pipes).
- Teflon (Polytetrafluoroethene, PTFE): Monomer is tetrafluoroethene ($\text{CF}_2=\text{CF}_2$); chemically inert, thermally stable up to $573\text{ K}$ (non-stick cookware, gaskets).
- Polyacrylonitrile (PAN / Orlon): Monomer is acrylonitrile ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH-CN}$); used as a wool substitute in commercial fibres.
- Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC): Monomer is vinyl chloride ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH-Cl}$); used in water pipes, raincoats, electrical conduits.

2.3 Natural vs. Synthetic Rubbers & Vulcanization

- Natural Rubber: Natural polymer of isoprene (2-methyl-1,3-butadiene):
   - Strictly cis-1,4-polyisoprene: Possesses a coiled spring-like conformation held by weak van der Waals forces, granting elastic recovery.
   - Gutta-Percha: The trans-1,4-polyisoprene stereoisomer; chains pack closely into a crystalline, rigid, non-elastic polymer.
- Vulcanization of Rubber:
   - Raw natural rubber is soft, sticky at high temperatures ($> 335\text{ K}$), brittle at low temperatures ($< 283\text{ K}$), and easily soluble in non-polar solvents.
   - Process: Heating raw rubber with $3-5%$ elemental sulfur ($S_8$) at $373-415\text{ K}$ in the presence of an accelerator.
   - Mechanism: Sulfur forms $-\text{S}-\text{S}-$ disulphide bridges across the reactive allylic double bonds of adjacent cis-polyisoprene chains, preventing permanent chain slippage and providing high elasticity, thermal resilience, and solvent resistance.
- Synthetic Rubbers:
   - Neoprene: Homopolymer of chloroprene (2-chloro-1,3-butadiene). Resistant to vegetable and mineral oils.
   - Buna-S (SBR): Copolymer of 1,3-butadiene ($75%$) and styrene ($25%$). High abrasion resistance; used for automobile tires.
   - Buna-N (NBR): Copolymer of 1,3-butadiene and acrylonitrile. Highly resistant to petroleum oils and organic solvents.

2.4 Visual Preservation: Vulcanization Structural Mechanism

 Description: Structural comparison of unvulcanized natural rubber chains (cis-1,4-polyisoprene) undergoing permanent sliding under strain versus vulcanized rubber where covalent disulphide cross-links ($-\text{S}-\text{S}-$) anchor adjacent chains, preventing slippage and restoring elasticity upon release of stress.

8. Condensation Resins (Step-Growth Polymers)

3.1 Polyamides

- Nylon-6,6:
   - Monomers: Hexamethylenediamine ($\text{H}_2\text{N-(CH}_2)_6\text{-NH}_2$) + Adipic acid ($\text{HOOC-(CH}_2)_4\text{-COOH}$).
   - Linked by amide bonds ($-\text{CO-NH}-$); high tensile strength due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
- Nylon-6:
   - Monomer: $\epsilon$-Caprolactam (seven-membered cyclic ring).
   - Prepared by heating caprolactam with water at $533-543\text{ K}$ via ring-opening polymerization.

3.2 Polyesters

- Dacron / Terylene:
   - Monomers: Ethylene glycol ($\text{HO-CH}_2\text{-CH}_2\text{-OH}$) + Terephthalic acid ($1,4\text{-benzene dicarboxylic acid}$).
   - Linked by ester bonds ($-\text{COO}-$); crease-resistant, blended with cotton (terycot) and wool (terywool).
- Glyptal (Alkyd Resin):
   - Monomers: Ethylene glycol + Phthalic anhydride ($1,2\text{-benzene dicarboxylic anhydride}$).
   - Cross-linked polyester used in paints, lacquers, and varnishes.

3.3 Phenol-Formaldehyde Resins

- Preparation: Reaction of phenol with formaldehyde in the presence of acid or base catalyst:
   1. Electrophilic aromatic substitution forms $o$- and $p$-hydroxymethylphenols.
   2. Condensation forms a linear polymer known as Novolac (used in paints).
   3. Further heating of Novolac with formaldehyde creates extensive cross-linking, producing the 3D infusible network Bakelite.

3.4 Visual Preservation: Bakelite 3D Network Architecture

 Description: Chemical structure of Bakelite highlighting the 3D cross-linked covalent network composed of phenol rings linked both horizontally and vertically through methylene ($-\text{CH}_2-$) bridges.

3.5 Melamine-Formaldehyde Resin

- Monomers: Melamine ($2,4,6\text{-triamino-1,3,5-triazine}$, a heterocyclic compound containing 6 nitrogen atoms) + Formaldehyde.
- Condensation polymerization yields an unplasticized thermosetting resin used in unbreakable crockery.

9. Biodegradable Polymers & Molecular Mass Distribution

4.1 Biodegradable Polymers

- Polymers degraded by environmental microorganisms into non-toxic degradation products:
   1. PHBV (Poly $\beta$-hydroxybutyrate-co-$\beta$-hydroxyvalerate):
      - Monomers: 3-hydroxybutanoic acid + 3-hydroxypentanoic acid.
      - Hydroxy acid copolymer containing ester linkages; undergoes bacterial degradation in the environment; used in specialty drug delivery and orthopedic devices.
   2. Nylon-2-Nylon-6:
      - Monomers: Glycine ($\text{H}_2\text{N-CH}_2\text{-COOH}$) + $\epsilon$-Aminocaproic acid ($\text{H}_2\text{N-(CH}_2)_5\text{-COOH}$).
      - Alternating polyamide copolymer with biodegradable amide linkages.

4.2 Molecular Mass Averages & Polydispersity Index ($PDI$)

- Unlike pure simple compounds, polymers contain chains of varying lengths and molecular weights:
   1. Number-Average Molecular Mass ($\bar{M}_n$): $$\bar{M}_n = \frac{\sum N_i M_i}{\sum N_i}$$
      - Determined experimentally via colligative property measurements (osmotic pressure, freezing point depression).
   2. Weight-Average Molecular Mass ($\bar{M}_w$): $$\bar{M}_w = \frac{\sum N_i M_i^2}{\sum N_i M_i} = \frac{\sum m_i M_i}{\sum m_i}$$
      - Determined experimentally via sedimentation, light scattering, and ultracentrifugation.
- Polydispersity Index ($PDI$): $$PDI = \frac{\bar{M}_w}{\bar{M}_n}$$
   - For monodisperse natural polymers (e.g., natural proteins, natural polysaccharides): $PDI = 1$ ($\bar{M}_w = \bar{M}_n$).
   - For polydisperse synthetic polymers: $PDI > 1$ ($\bar{M}_w > \bar{M}_n$ due to distribution of chain lengths).
