1. Group 13 Elements: The Boron Family
1.1 General Characteristics & Electronic Structure
* Members & Valence Configurations:
   * Boron ($\text{B}$, $Z=5$): $[\text{He}], 2s^2 2p^1$ (Non-metal, refractory high-melting covalent solid)
   * Aluminium ($\text{Al}$, $Z=13$): $[\text{Ne}], 3s^2 3p^1$ (Metal, high electropositivity)
   * Gallium ($\text{Ga}$, $Z=31$): $[\text{Ar}], 3d^{10} 4s^2 4p^1$ (Low melting metal: $30^\circ\text{C}$, expands upon freezing)
   * Indium ($\text{In}$, $Z=49$): $[\text{Kr}], 4d^{10} 5s^2 5p^1$ (Soft metal)
   * Thallium ($\text{Tl}$, $Z=81$): $[\text{Xe}], 4f^{14} 5d^{10} 6s^2 6p^1$ (Heavy metal dominated by $+1$ oxidation state)
1.2 Anomalous Atomic & Physical Trends
1. Atomic Radii Anomaly ($\text{Al} \approx \text{Ga}$):
   * Expected: $\text{B} < \text{Al} < \text{Ga} < \text{In} < \text{Tl}$.
   * Experimental Reality: $r(\text{Al}) \approx 143\text{ pm} > r(\text{Ga}) \approx 135\text{ pm}$.
   * Origin: Gallium is preceded by the ten $3d$ transition elements. The diffuse $3d$ electrons provide poor nuclear shielding, resulting in a sharply increased effective nuclear charge ($Z_{\text{eff}}$), contracting the atomic radius (Transition Contraction or d-block contraction).
2. Ionization Enthalpy Hierarchy ($\Delta_i H$):
   * Discontinuous irregular order: $$\mathbf{\text{B} > \text{Tl} > \text{Ga} > \text{Al} > \text{In}}$$
   * $\text{Ga} > \text{Al}$ due to $3d$ transition contraction.
   * $\text{Tl} > \text{In}$ due to the intervening fourteen $4f$ electrons exhibiting poor shielding (Lanthanide Contraction), binding the $6s^2$ electrons tightly.
3. Electronegativity Order (Pauling Scale):
   * $\mathbf{\text{B} (2.0) > \text{Tl} (1.8) > \text{In} (1.7) = \text{Ga} (1.6) > \text{Al} (1.5)}$.
   * Decreases from $\text{B}$ to $\text{Al}$, then slightly increases due to imperfect shielding of $d$ and $f$ electrons.
4. Melting & Boiling Points:
   * Melting Point: $\text{B} (2453\text{ K}) > \text{Al} (933\text{ K}) > \text{Tl} (577\text{ K}) > \text{In} (430\text{ K}) > \text{Ga} (303\text{ K})$.
   * Gallium melts in the hand ($30^\circ\text{C}$) but has a very high boiling point ($2676\text{ K}$), giving it the largest liquid range of any element; hence it is used in high-temperature quartz thermometers.
1.3 Oxidation States & The Inert Pair Effect
* Outer configuration $ns^2 np^1$.
* Inert Pair Effect: Reluctance of the valence $ns^2$ electrons to participate in bonding due to poor shielding by inner $(n-1)d^{10}$ and $(n-2)f^{14}$ electrons, which causes the outer $s$-electrons to penetrate closer to the nucleus.
* Stability Trends:
   * $+3$ state stability: $\mathbf{\text{B}^{3+} > \text{Al}^{3+} > \text{Ga}^{3+} > \text{In}^{3+} > \text{Tl}^{3+}}$.
   * $+1$ state stability: $\mathbf{\text{Tl}^+ > \text{In}^+ > \text{Ga}^+ > \text{Al}^+}$.
* Redox Invariants:
   * $\text{Tl}^{3+}$ is a powerful oxidizing agent, readily converting to stable $\text{Tl}^+$: $$\text{Tl}^{3+} + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Tl}^+ \quad (E^\circ = +1.26\text{ V})$$
   * Compounds of $\text{Tl}^+$ resemble alkali metal salts ($\text{TlOH}$ is a strong water-soluble base like $\text{KOH}$; $\text{TlCl}$ is an insoluble white solid resembling $\text{AgCl}$).

2. Boron & Its High-Yield Compounds
![Diborane 3c2e Bonding And Borax Structure](/media/diborane_3c2e_bonding_and_borax_structure.webp) Description: Two-panel reference diagram: (Panel A) Diborane ($B_2H_6$) showing four terminal 2c-2e $B-H_t$ covalent bonds and two 3c-2e banana bridges ($B-H_b-B$) with bond angles and lengths; (Panel B) Borax core anion $[B_4O_5(OH)_4]^{2-}$ illustrating two tetrahedral $sp^3$ boron centers and two planar $sp^2$ boron centers bridged by five $B-O-B$ linkages.
2.1 Diborane ($\text{B}_2\text{H}_6$)
1. Synthesis Pathways:
   * Industrial method (reduction of boron trifluoride by sodium hydride): $$2\text{BF}_3 + 6\text{NaH} \xrightarrow{450\text{ K}} \text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 6\text{NaF}$$
   * Laboratory preparation: $$4\text{BCl}_3 + 3\text{LiAlH}_4 \xrightarrow{\text{Dry Ether}} 2\text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 3\text{LiCl} + 3\text{AlCl}_3$$ $$2\text{NaBH}_4 + \text{I}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Diglyme}} \text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 2\text{NaI} + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$$
2. Molecular Geometry & Electronic Structure:
   * Contains 12 valence electrons (3 from each of two Borons, 1 from each of six Hydrogens). Monomeric $\text{BH}_3$ has only 6 electrons and is electron-deficient.
   * Four terminal Hydrogens ($\text{H}_t$) form classical two-center two-electron ($2c-2e$) bonds: length $= 1.19\text{ \AA}$, $\angle \text{H}_t-\text{B}-\text{H}_t = 120^\circ$.
   * Two bridging Hydrogens ($\text{H}_b$) lie above and below the molecular plane, forming three-center two-electron ($3c-2e$) "banana" bonds: length $= 1.31\text{ \AA}$, $\angle \text{B}-\text{H}_b-\text{B} = 97^\circ$.
   * Both Boron atoms are $sp^3$ hybridized.
3. Key Chemical Reactions:
   * Combustion (Extremely Exothermic): $$\text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 3\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{B}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\Delta H = -1976\text{ kJ/mol})$$
   * Hydrolysis: $$\text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3 + 6\text{H}_2 \uparrow$$
   * Reaction with Alcohol: $$\text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 6\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \longrightarrow 2\text{B}(\text{OC}_2\text{H}_5)_3 + 6\text{H}_2 \uparrow$$ (Triethyl borate burns with a characteristic green-edged flame).
   * Cleavage Reactions with Lewis Bases:
      * Symmetrical Cleavage (Large/Soft bases: $\text{CO}, \text{Me}_3\text{N}, \text{THF}, \text{R}_2\text{S}$): $$\text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 2\text{NMe}_3 \longrightarrow 2[\text{Me}_3\text{N} \to \text{BH}_3]$$ $$\text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 2\text{CO} \longrightarrow 2[\text{OC} \to \text{BH}_3]$$
      * Unsymmetrical Cleavage (Small/Hard bases: $\text{NH}_3, \text{MeNH}_2, \text{Me}_2\text{NH}$): $$\text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 2\text{NH}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{Low Temp}} [\text{BH}_2(\text{NH}_3)_2]^+ [\text{BH}_4]^-$$
2.2 Borazole / Inorganic Benzene ($\text{B}_3\text{N}_3\text{H}_6$)
* Synthesis: Heating the unsymmetrical cleavage adduct at $200^\circ\text{C}$: $$3\text{B}_2\text{H}_6 + 6\text{NH}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} 2\text{B}_3\text{N}_3\text{H}_6 + 12\text{H}_2 \uparrow$$ $$3\text{BCl}_3 + 3\text{NH}_4\text{Cl} \longrightarrow \text{B}_3\text{N}_3\text{H}_3\text{Cl}_3 + 9\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{\text{NaBH}_4} \text{B}_3\text{N}_3\text{H}_6$$
* Structure & Comparison with Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$):
   * Hexagonal planar ring with alternating $\text{B}$ and $\text{N}$ atoms; both $\text{B}$ and $\text{N}$ are $sp^2$ hybridized.
   * Isoelectronic and isostructural with benzene, containing $6\pi$ delocalized electrons.
   * Differences: Due to electronegativity difference ($\chi_{\text{N}} > \chi_{\text{B}}$), the $\text{B}-\text{N}$ bond is polar ($\text{B}^{\delta+} - \text{N}^{\delta-}$).
   * Reactivity: Borazole undergoes electrophilic and nucleophilic additions much more readily than benzene.
      * Addition of $\text{HCl}$: $$\text{B}_3\text{N}_3\text{H}_6 + 3\text{HCl} \longrightarrow \text{B}_3\text{N}_3\text{H}_9\text{Cl}_3$$ (Hydrogen adds to more electronegative Nitrogen; Chlorine adds to Boron).
      * Hydrolysis: $$\text{B}_3\text{N}_3\text{H}_6 + 9\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 3\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3 + 3\text{NH}_3 + 3\text{H}_2 \uparrow$$
2.3 Borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$)
* True Structural Formula: $\mathbf{\text{Na}_2[\text{B}_4\text{O}_5(\text{OH})_4] \cdot 8\text{H}_2\text{O}}$.
   * Contains a bicyclic core consisting of two tetrahedral $\text{BO}_4$ units ($sp^3$ hybridized, formal charge $-1$) and two triangular planar $\text{BO}_3$ units ($sp^2$ hybridized, neutral).
   * Number of $\text{B}-\text{O}-\text{B}$ linkages $= 5$.
   * Number of $\text{B}-\text{OH}$ units $= 4$.
* Aqueous Chemistry: $$\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 + 7\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NaOH} + 4\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3$$
   * Forms a buffer solution of a strong base ($\text{NaOH}$) and a weak acid ($\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3$); hence its aqueous solution is alkaline ($\text{pH} > 7$).
* Borax Bead Test: $$\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \xrightarrow{\text{Red Heat}} \underbrace{2\text{NaBO}_2 + \text{B}_2\text{O}3}{\text{Transparent Glassy Bead}}$$
   * When heated with colored transition metal salts, forms characteristic metaborates:
      * Cobalt ($\text{Co}^{2+}$): $\text{Co}(\text{BO}_2)_2$ $\to$ Deep Blue (in both oxidizing and reducing flames).
      * Copper ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$): $\text{Cu}(\text{BO}_2)_2$ $\to$ Blue-Green (oxidizing flame), turns red opaque ($\text{Cu}$) in reducing flame.
      * Chromium ($\text{Cr}^{3+}$): $\text{Cr}(\text{BO}_2)_3$ $\to$ Green.
      * Iron ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$): $\text{Fe}(\text{BO}_2)_3$ $\to$ Yellow/Brown.
2.4 Orthoboric Acid ($\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3$ or $\text{B}(\text{OH})_3$)
* Acidic Nature:
   * It is not a protonic acid (does not donate $\text{H}^+$ by itself).
   * It acts as a weak, monobasic Lewis acid by accepting an electron pair from the $\text{OH}^-$ of a water molecule: $$\text{B}(\text{OH})_3 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons [\text{B}(\text{OH})_4]^- + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ \quad (\text{p}K_a = 9.25)$$
* Titration with $\text{NaOH}$ & Polyhydroxy Activation:
   * Cannot be directly titrated with $\text{NaOH}$ using phenolphthalein because its dissociation constant is too small.
   * In the presence of cis-polyols (glycerol, mannitol, catechol, sugars), it forms stable, rigid cyclic chelate complexes with $[\text{B}(\text{OH})_4]^-$, shifting the equilibrium forward and converting it into a strong monobasic acid that can be titrated quantitatively with phenolphthalein.
* Thermal Decomposition: $$\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3 \xrightarrow{373\text{ K}} \text{HBO}_2 \text{ (Metaboric acid)} \xrightarrow{413\text{ K}} \text{H}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 \text{ (Tetraboric acid)} \xrightarrow{\text{Red Heat}} \text{B}_2\text{O}_3 \text{ (Boric oxide)}$$
2.5 Boron Trihalides ($\text{BX}_3$) & Back-Bonding
* Lewis Acidity Order: $$\mathbf{\text{BF}_3 < \text{BCl}_3 < \text{BBr}_3 < \text{BI}_3}$$
* Explanation:
   * Boron has an empty $2p$ orbital in planar $sp^2$ hybridized $\text{BX}_3$.
   * Halogens donate lone pairs from their filled $np$ orbitals into Boron's empty $2p$ orbital via $p\pi-p\pi$ back-bonding.
   * Overlap efficiency depends on orbital size matching: $$2p_\pi(\text{B}) - 2p_\pi(\text{F}) > 2p_\pi(\text{B}) - 3p_\pi(\text{Cl}) > 2p_\pi(\text{B}) - 4p_\pi(\text{Br}) > 2p_\pi(\text{B}) - 5p_\pi(\text{I})$$
   * In $\text{BF}_3$, maximum back-bonding relieves the electron deficiency of Boron, making it the weakest Lewis acid. In $\text{BI}_3$, poor $2p-5p$ overlap results in negligible back-bonding, making it the strongest Lewis acid.
* Hydrolysis Behavior:
   * $\text{BCl}_3, \text{BBr}_3, \text{BI}_3$ undergo complete, irreversible hydrolysis: $$\text{BCl}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{H}_3\text{BO}_3 + 3\text{HCl}$$
   * $\text{BF}_3$ hydrolyzes partially because released $\text{HF}$ coordinates with unreacted $\text{BF}_3$ to form fluoroboric acid: $$4\text{BF}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{H}_3\text{BO}_3 + 3\text{H}[\text{BF}_4]$$

3. Group 14 Elements: The Carbon Family
3.1 Electronic Configuration & Physical Properties
* Valence shell configuration: $ns^2 np^2$.
* Elements: Carbon ($\text{C}$, non-metal), Silicon ($\text{Si}$, metalloid), Germanium ($\text{Ge}$, metalloid), Tin ($\text{Sn}$, metal), Lead ($\text{Pb}$, metal).
* Catenation Power:
   * Property of forming long covalent chains and rings. Decreases rapidly down the group as bond energy decreases: $$\mathbf{\text{C} \gg \text{Si} > \text{Ge} \approx \text{Sn} \quad (\text{Pb shows zero catenation})}$$ $$\text{C}-\text{C } (348\text{ kJ/mol}) > \text{Si}-\text{Si } (297\text{ kJ/mol}) > \text{Ge}-\text{Ge } (260\text{ kJ/mol}) > \text{Sn}-\text{Sn } (240\text{ kJ/mol})$$
* Oxidation States & Redox Behavior:
   * $+4$ and $+2$ oxidation states exist.
   * Inert pair effect stabilizes $+2$ for heavier elements:
      * $+2$ Stability: $\mathbf{\text{Ge}^{2+} < \text{Sn}^{2+} < \text{Pb}^{2+}}$.
      * $+4$ Stability: $\mathbf{\text{C}^{4+} > \text{Si}^{4+} > \text{Ge}^{4+} > \text{Sn}^{4+} > \text{Pb}^{4+}}$.
   * Consequences:
      * $\text{Sn}^{2+}$ is a reducing agent ($\text{Sn}^{2+} \to \text{Sn}^{4+} + 2e^-$).
      * $\text{Pb}^{4+}$ is a strong oxidizing agent ($\text{Pb}^{4+} + 2e^- \to \text{Pb}^{2+}$), meaning $\text{PbO}_2$ and $\text{PbCl}_4$ easily reduce to $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ salts. $\text{PbI}_4$ does not exist because $\text{I}^-$ reduces $\text{Pb}^{4+}$.

	4.2 Fullerene Topology Rules
* In $\text{C}_{60}$, there are 20 six-membered rings (hexagons) and 12 five-membered rings (pentagons).
* Euler's Polyhedral Invariant: A closed fullerene cage composed of pentagons and hexagons must contain exactly 12 pentagons, regardless of the total number of carbon atoms: $$N_{\text{pentagons}} = 12, \qquad N_{\text{hexagons}} = \frac{n - 20}{2} \quad (\text{for } \text{C}_n)$$
* Isolated Pentagon Rule (IPR): No two pentagons share an edge in stable fullerenes. Each pentagon is fused exclusively to five hexagons. Hexagons are fused to both hexagons and pentagons.

5. Oxides & Halides of Carbon and Silicon
5.1 Carbon Monoxide ($\text{CO}$)
* Preparation:
   * Dehydration of formic acid by concentrated sulphuric acid: $$\text{HCOOH} \xrightarrow{\text{conc. }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4, \Delta} \text{CO} \uparrow + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
   * Industrial producer gas: $2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 + 4\text{N}_2 \to 2\text{CO} + 4\text{N}_2$; Water gas (syngas): $\text{C} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{CO} + \text{H}_2$.
* Electronic Structure: $:C \equiv O:$ with a coordinate covalent bond from $\text{O}$ to $\text{C}$. Acts as a strong $\sigma$-donor and $\pi$-acceptor ligand, forming toxic carboxyhemoglobin (300 times more stable than oxyhemoglobin).
5.2 Carbon Dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) & Carbon Suboxide ($\text{C}_3\text{O}_2$)
* $\text{CO}_2$: Linear ($O=C=O$), non-polar ($\mu = 0$), weak acid forming carbonic acid $\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$ in water.
* Carbon Suboxide ($\text{C}_3\text{O}_2$):
   * Obtained by dehydrating malonic acid with phosphorus pentoxide: $$\text{CH}_2(\text{COOH})_2 \xrightarrow{\text{P}4\text{O}{10}, 150^\circ\text{C}} \text{O}=\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{C}=\text{O} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
   * Linear cumulative double-bond structure, toxic foul-smelling gas.
5.3 Silicon Tetrachloride ($\text{SiCl}_4$) vs. Carbon Tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$)
* $\text{CCl}_4$ is completely inert to hydrolysis under ambient conditions because Carbon has no low-lying vacant $d$-orbitals to accept a lone pair from attacking $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules.
* $\text{SiCl}_4$ hydrolyzes violently in water because Silicon can expand its coordination number by utilizing empty $3d$-orbitals: $$\text{SiCl}_4 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{Si}(\text{OH})_4 + 4\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{SiO}_2 \text{ (Silicic acid gel)}$$

6. Silicates & Silicones
![Silicates Classification And Silicones Polymerization](/media/silicates_classification_and_silicones_polymerization.webp) Description: Two-panel reference diagram: (Panel A) Comprehensive classification of silicates (ortho, pyro, cyclic, single chain, amphibole double chain, sheet, and 3D tektosilicates) detailing basic structural units, shared corner oxygens per tetrahedron, and mineral examples; (Panel B) Silicones industrial synthesis via Direct Process, controlled hydrolysis of chlorosilanes, chain termination, propagation, and cross-linking chemistry.
6.1 Classification of Silicates
All natural silicates are built from the fundamental tetrahedral structural unit $\mathbf{[\text{SiO}_4]^{4-}}$, where Silicon is $sp^3$ hybridized with a coordination number of 4.


1. Orthosilicates (Nesosilicates):
   * Structural unit: $[\text{SiO}_4]^{4-}$.
   * Shared oxygen vertices per tetrahedron: 0.
   * Examples: Phenacite ($\text{Be}_2\text{SiO}_4$), Willemite ($\text{Zn}_2\text{SiO}_4$), Forsterite ($\text{Mg}_2\text{SiO}_4$).
2. Pyrosilicates (Sorosilicates / Island Silicates):
   * Structural unit: $[\text{Si}_2\text{O}_7]^{6-}$.
   * Shared oxygen vertices: 1 corner oxygen atom shared between two tetrahedra.
   * Examples: Thortveitite ($\text{Sc}_2\text{Si}_2\text{O}_7$), Hemimorphite.
3. Cyclic / Ring Silicates:
   * General formula: $[(\text{SiO}_3)_n]^{2n-}$.
   * Shared oxygen vertices: 2 corners per tetrahedron.
   * Prominent examples:
      * Trimer ($n=3$): $[\text{Si}_3\text{O}_9]^{6-}$ in Wollastonite ($\text{Ca}_3\text{Si}_3\text{O}_9$).
      * Hexamer ($n=6$): $[\text{Si}6\text{O}{18}]^{12-}$ in Beryl ($\text{Be}_3\text{Al}_2\text{Si}6\text{O}{18}$).
4. Chain Silicates (Inosilicates):
   * Single Chain (Pyroxenes): Formula $[(\text{SiO}_3)_n]^{2n-}$, each tetrahedron shares 2 oxygen atoms with neighbors. Example: Diopside ($\text{CaMg}(\text{SiO}_3)_2$).
   * Double Chain (Amphiboles): Formula $[(\text{Si}4\text{O}{11})_n]^{6n-}$, tetrahedra alternately share 2 and 3 oxygen atoms (average $2.5$ shared corners). Example: Tremolite ($\text{Ca}_2\text{Mg}_5(\text{Si}4\text{O}{11})_2(\text{OH})_2$), Asbestos minerals.
5. Sheet Silicates (Phyllosilicates):
   * Structural unit: $[(\text{Si}_2\text{O}_5)_n]^{2n-}$.
   * Shared oxygen vertices: 3 corners per tetrahedron, forming a continuous 2D planar infinite network.
   * Examples: Talc ($\text{Mg}_3(\text{Si}_2\text{O}_5)_2(\text{OH})_2$, softest mineral), Kaolinite ($\text{Al}_2(\text{OH})_4(\text{Si}_2\text{O}_5)$), Mica.
6. Three-Dimensional / Framework Silicates (Tektosilicates):
   * Structural unit: $(\text{SiO}_2)_n$.
   * Shared oxygen vertices: All 4 corners shared with adjacent tetrahedra, forming a 3D covalent network.
   * Examples: Quartz, Tridymite, Cristobalite, Feldspars.
   * Zeolites: Microporous aluminosilicates where some $\text{Si}^{4+}$ ions in the 3D network are replaced by $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ions. The negative charge is balanced by exchangeable cations ($\text{Na}^+, \text{K}^+, \text{Ca}^{2+}$). Used as shape-selective catalysts (ZSM-5 converts alcohols directly into gasoline) and for softening hard water (Permutit process).
6.2 Silicones
Organosilicon polymers having general repeating units $\mathbf{[-R_2\text{Si}-\text{O}-]_n}$.


1. Synthesis Pathways:
   * Reaction of chloromethane with silicon in the presence of copper catalyst at $570\text{ K}$ (Direct Process): $$2\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{Si} \xrightarrow{\text{Cu}, 570\text{ K}} (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{SiCl}_2 \text{ (major)} + \text{CH}_3\text{SiCl}_3 + (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{SiCl} + \text{SiCl}_4$$
2. Controlled Polymerization:
   * Chain Propagator ($R_2\text{SiCl}_2$): Hydrolysis gives silanols that condense to form linear silicone polymers: $$(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{SiCl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{Si}(\text{OH})_2 + 2\text{HCl}$$ $$n(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{Si}(\text{OH})_2 \xrightarrow{-\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{---}\left[\text{Si}(\text{CH}_3)_2-\text{O}\right]_n\text{---}$$
   * Chain Stopper / Terminator ($R_3\text{SiCl}$): Monochlorosilane has only one hydrolyzable site, capping chain growth: $$2(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{SiCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{SiOH} \xrightarrow{-\text{H}_2\text{O}} (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{Si}-\text{O}-\text{Si}(\text{CH}_3)_3$$ (Used to regulate molecular weight and chain length).
   * Cross-Linking Agent ($R\text{SiCl}_3$): Trichlorosilane yields trihydroxy intermediates that cross-link adjacent chains into rigid 3D resins: $$\text{CH}_3\text{SiCl}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Si}(\text{OH})_3 + 3\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{-\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{Complex 3D Cross-linked Resins}$$
3. Properties & Applications:
   * Highly resistant to oxidation and thermal degradation due to strong $\text{Si}-\text{O}$ backbone bonds ($\sim 452\text{ kJ/mol}$).
   * Surrounded by non-polar organic alkyl groups, rendering them water-repellent (hydrophobic).
   * Excellent electrical insulators (high dielectric strength).
   * Viscosity remains almost constant over wide temperature ranges (used as lubricants in high-altitude aircraft).

7. High-Yield JEE Traps & Exam Invariants
1. Gallium Expansion Trap:
   * Gallium expands by $3.1\%$ upon solidification. It should not be stored in glass or metal containers as it can break them upon freezing.
2. $\text{TlI}_3$ Formulation Trap:
   * $\text{TlI}_3$ is not a compound of $\text{Tl}^{3+}$. Due to the inert pair effect and the strong reducing power of iodide, it exists as an ionic compound containing thallous ion and triiodide ion: $$\mathbf{\text{TlI}_3 \equiv \text{Tl}^+ [\text{I}_3]^- = \text{Tl}^+ [\text{I}-\text{I}-\text{I}]^-}$$
3. Boric Acid Acidity Trap:
   * $\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3$ does not dissociate as $\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3 \to \text{H}^+ + \text{H}_2\text{BO}_3^-$. It extracts $\text{OH}^-$ from water, acting as a monobasic Lewis acid, not a Brønsted acid.
4. Borax Hybridization Ratio:
   * In crystalline borax $[\text{B}_4\text{O}_5(\text{OH})_4]^{2-}$, exactly two Boron atoms are $sp^2$ hybridized and two Boron atoms are $sp^3$ hybridized (ratio $1:1$).
5. Diborane Hydrogen Exchange Trap:
   * When $\text{B}_2\text{H}_6$ is treated with excess deuterium gas ($\text{D}_2$), only the four terminal hydrogens exchange rapidly; the two bridging hydrogens exchange extremely slowly. Methylation of $\text{B}_2\text{H}_6$ produces tetramethyldiborane $(\text{CH}_3)_4\text{B}_2\text{H}_2$—bridging hydrogens cannot be methylated without cleaving the dimer.
6. Carbon Monoxide Coordinate Bond Trap:
   * In $\text{CO}$, despite Oxygen being more electronegative than Carbon, the net dipole moment is very small ($0.11\text{ D}$) with the negative pole residing on Carbon, because the coordinate bond donates electron density from Oxygen to Carbon.
7. Lead Halides Stability Trap:
   * $\text{PbI}_4$ does not exist (instantaneous reduction: $\text{Pb}^{4+} + 2\text{I}^- \to \text{Pb}^{2+} + \text{I}_2$).
   * $\text{PbF}_4$ and $\text{PbCl}_4$ exist because fluorine and chlorine cannot reduce $\text{Pb}^{4+}$.
